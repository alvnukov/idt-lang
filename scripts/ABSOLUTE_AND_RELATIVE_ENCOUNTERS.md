# Do the packets need to know the original blank?

Two reversible three-packet candidates agree on *every* old calibration
with two blank packets, including the inverse of each active command.
The second law refers only to two packets being at the same record, not
to their being at a distinguished absolute blank. If the third differs
on just one port of each block, all three take the step holding the
differing large port. It is covariant under all simultaneous translations
of the input records. The anchor-dependent law is not.

Of 4,096 ordered triples the relative law acts on 288; the anchored
law acts on 36. The **entire** subset of the 288 preserving the earlier
ballot sum consists of exactly those 36. Thus the anchored law is the
maximal ballot-preserving restriction *within this particular relative
encounter*. Ballot preservation, absolute root hardware, and the relative
law itself are three different hypotheses. None has been observed here.

| Preparation in one fixed frame | Read after one command | Anchored | Relative |
| --- | --- | ---: | ---: |
| Two blanks and a record differing in one port of each block | Number at the known hopped blank | 2 | 2 |
| Two packets at the retained mark, same relative difference on the third | Number still at the mark | 2 | 0 |

Keep the reader in place. Moving the reader together with the preparation
is a change of coordinates, not the second intervention. The first row
is a firing control: no motion at the second setting without that control
could also be an idle device. Reading a third, unused tag can return
zero for both laws, even when their output configurations differ.

The `ternary_encounter` adapter compares integer tagged counts only.
Its ten preserved designs include both mutually exclusive outcomes of
the proposed experiment. They are **not two measurements**, and neither
physical candidate has been eliminated. The adapter uses one comparison
per submitted count, reports a budget-limited run as inconclusive, and
does not infer a charge, frequency law, or detector calibration.

If even the absolute origin is unknown but remains fixed across trials,
use two disjoint queries. Prepare `(0,0,9)` then `(3,3,10)`, asking
whether the equal pair left its tag each time. Both leave under the
relative rule; no single anchored origin can cause both departures.
An idle device still needs the separate functioning control.

Calibrating *which* of the 16 origins is present is harder than this
two-law test. For a fixed partition, each informative tag query asks
whether the origin is one of two adjacent possibilities. Those pairs
form two disjoint eight-vertex cubes. The finite search and constructive
protocol reach the exact minima **8 adaptive queries in the worst case,
or 11 with all queries fixed in advance**. There are 160 distinct anchored
behaviors when both origin and partition vary, versus ten relative ones.
These are behavioral distinctions under a common external tagging system,
not proof that a material apparatus must contain eight memory cells.
